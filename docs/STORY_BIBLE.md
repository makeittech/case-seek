## 1. Logline, Tone & Themes

### Logline

On the eve of its triumphant unveiling, the celebrated 1904 painting *The Cartographer's Daughter* vanishes from a locked gallery, leaving its frame hanging hollow. An insurance detective with a good eye and a bad hat discovers the theft is not an acquisition but a **cover-up**: the museum's masterpiece was a copy all along, painted last year by a dead man — and the thief stole it back to keep anyone from looking too closely.

### Tone

Cozy-noir, exactly as the GDD locks it: warm lamplight, dry wit, real stakes, no gore, no horror. The darkest beat is a betrayal, not a body. Model: a well-made radio drama with a fondness for tea. Every character, including the culprit, is allowed dignity and at least one good joke.

### Themes

1. **What makes the original original?** A copy of a Vane, painted by Vane — is it a forgery or a second masterpiece? The season's central argument, resolved humanely, not academically.
2. **Attention is a form of love.** Wren's only power is *looking properly*. The searches are the theme made playable: the world rewards whoever pays attention — which is also how one learns a language.
3. **Frames.** The hollow frame; the framing of Casal; the frames people build around their reputations. Finale line (canonical): *"Frames don't hold paintings, Miss Voss. People do."*
4. **Two birds.** The detective is a wren (small, plain, sharp-eyed, sings anyway). The antagonist is a nightingale (celebrated voice, never seen, sings only at night). The season is the wren out-singing the nightingale — by daylight, in a notebook.

## 2. The Protagonist — Wren Halloway

**Name:** Wren Halloway. Most people say "Halloway." Exactly three characters ever use "Wren," each meaningfully (Margo, once earned; Nachtigall, unsettlingly early; Vane, at the end).
**Age:** 38. **Former life:** eleven years a loss adjuster for the Aldermere Assurance Co. — cataloguing sunk cargo, burnt warehouses, and human excuses — before going independent. The insurers still send the interesting jobs.
**Presentation:** the player *is* Halloway. Scripts address the player in second person; other characters say "Halloway." No portrait dependency, no pronoun dependency in dialogue — the classic behind-the-eyes detective. Flavor-choice beats (two options, GDD rule, no plot branching) let the player color Halloway drier or warmer.

### Character (observant, warm, slightly eccentric — never a superhero)

- **Observant as a habit, not a gift.** Halloway has no deductive lightning. Halloway has *notebooks* — forty-one of them, numbered — and the patience to fill them. The genius, such as it is, is refusing to stop looking one hour before everyone else does.
- **Warm.** Remembers the pawnbroker's lumbago. Feeds the clocktower pigeons before searching the clocktower. Believes almost everyone is lying and likes them anyway.
- **Eccentric, gently.** Labels everything — jars, drawers, the cat that isn't Halloway's cat (*"Witness"*). Collects words the way other people collect stamps; this is the diegetic soul of the WORDS tab: *"A word a day keeps the fog off."* Sketches strangers' shoes, because shoes don't lie. Tea strong enough to stand a spoon in.
- **Not a superhero.** Cannot whistle. Gets seasick on a wet pontoon (paid off comically at the Ferry Pier). Loses the same grey hat approximately once per chapter. Never throws a punch all season; when the Corbin brothers turn violent in Chapter 6, it is Inspector Holt's constables who handle it, as it should be.
- **The languages.** Halloway's ear for the port's tongues is professional humility: *"People tell the truth in the language they shop in."* The study notebook (the player's vocabulary journal) is Halloway's own — the fiction and the feature are the same object.

### Voice samples

- *"The frame was hung by someone who loved it. That narrows things wonderfully."*
- *"I don't believe in hunches. I believe in inventory."*
- (Flavor pair at a key beat) — DRY: *"Madam, I've been thrown out of nicer museums."* / WARM: *"Take your time. The truth's been waiting since 1904; it can wait for your tea."*

## 3. The Companion System — Margo Lin

The GDD's cast is locked at eight speakers; the language companion is therefore **not** a ninth character but a *system built into Margo Lin*, the ally-journalist. One portrait set, one narrative slot, three language skins.

### How Margo adapts to the study language

Margo's father's people gave her the surname; her **mother's mother raised her in the Lantern Quarter**, Marlowe Bay's lamplit market district — home of the port's German, Spanish, or Italian community *depending on the player's study language*. Her grandmother adapts accordingly:

| Study language | Grandmother | Quarter flavor (signage, vendors, proverbs) |
|---|---|---|
| German | **Oma Katja** | Bremen-and-Baltic port stock; brass, rye bread, precise proverbs |
| Spanish | **Abuela Rosa** | Andalusian-and-Atlantic stock; azulejo tiles, oranges, singing vendors |
| Italian | **Nonna Lucia** | Ligurian stock; laundry lines, basil pots, operatic argument as recreation |

Everything else about Margo — biography, beats, jokes, function — is identical across languages. Content cost: one set of dialogue files with **token slots**, plus localized Lantern Quarter signage layers (flavor only, never required reading).

### Code-switching rules (the garnish system, GDD §8.3 compliant)

The GDD locks dialogue garnish to **none at New/Basics, greetings/interjections at Conversational, idioms at Advanced**. Margo is the primary carrier of that garnish. Her lines are authored once with three rendering levels:

- **L0 (New/Basics):** fully English — but her *culture* still shows: grandmother's proverbs quoted in English ("Oma Katja used to say: who searches, finds"), quarter references, food, songs. Identity without vocabulary load.
- **L1 (Conversational):** discourse markers, greetings, exclamations — one non-English token per line, tap-to-gloss, never load-bearing.
- **L2 (Advanced):** short idioms and clauses, tap-to-gloss, still never plot-critical.

**Authoring rules for every code-switch:**
1. Switch at emotion peaks and transitions (greeting, alarm, triumph, tenderness) — where real bilinguals switch. Never mid-fact, never on clue content.
2. **Never classroom English.** Margo does not translate herself, does not say "which means…", does not slow down. The tap-gloss does the teaching; Margo just talks.
3. **The echo rule:** within two beats of a search, Margo re-uses one or two of the round's taught nouns naturally ("You found the *Schlüssel*? Good — door number two won't pick itself"). This is the invisible review loop wearing a trench coat.
4. Comprehension of any Margo line must survive deleting the non-English tokens.

### Sample lines (same beat, three languages, L1/L2)

*Beat: Margo talks the pair past the Grand Hotel concierge (Ch. 3).*

- **German L1:** "*Also* — the concierge owes me a favor. *Na ja*, three favors, but who's counting?"
- **German L2:** "*Pass auf* — smile, say nothing, and follow the luggage. *Da liegt der Hund begraben*, as Oma Katja says. That's where the dog's buried — Suite 412."
- **Spanish L1:** "*Bueno* — the concierge owes me a favor. *Venga*, look expensive."
- **Spanish L2:** "*Ojo*, Halloway — smile, say nothing, follow the luggage. Abuela Rosa always said *el que busca, encuentra*. So let's go *encontrar*."
- **Italian L1:** "*Allora* — the concierge owes me a favor. *Dai*, look expensive."
- **Italian L2:** "*Occhio* — smile, say nothing, follow the luggage. Nonna Lucia says *chi cerca trova*. So — *cerca*."

*Bird garnish (Ch. 4, after Nachtigall's note calls Halloway "little wren"):*
- German L2: "In Oma's tongue a wren is a *Zaunkönig* — 'king of the fence.' Cheeky little thing, perches on other people's property. Suits you."
- Spanish L2: "Abuela called wrens the loudest small thing God made. She'd have liked you."
- Italian L2: "*Lo scricciolo* — the wren. Tiny bird, enormous opinions. Nonna Lucia kept one. It bullied the cat."

### Other language surfaces (system-owned, tier-gated per GDD)

- **Vendors' calls** (Ch. 4 audio round): the Lantern Quarter market *is* Margo's quarter; the "follow the vendors' calls" round is diegetically hers.
- **Vane's sketchbook caption** (Ch. 5, tap-to-gloss): *„Die Nachtigall singt noch." / «El ruiseñor todavía canta.» / «L'usignolo canta ancora.»* — "The nightingale still sings."
- **Nachtigall's alias:** the word itself is German for nightingale in all three language runs (it is her historical staff nickname, fixed in 1907 — see §6); Margo glosses the bird-word in the study language when the alias first surfaces.

## 4. The Cast — Dossiers, Secrets, Lies

Eight speakers, per the GDD. Named non-speakers (letters, ledgers, gestures only): Mr. Pettibone of Aldermere Assurance; "the Chalk" (the Anchor's bookmaker — literally a chalkboard); Dr. Lorentz (telegrams); the Corbin brothers (hired muscle); the boathouse keeper; Aurelian Marsh (d. 1911).

### The Detective — Wren Halloway
See §2. Status arc: outsider → Holt's grudging equal → the person everyone in the epilogue writes letters to.

### Adele Voss — curator, quest-giver, red herring
- **Surface:** 34, brilliant, brittle-composed; the 1926 acquisition of *The Cartographer's Daughter* was her career coup; commissioned the unveiling.
- **Secret (three layers, peeled across the season):** (1) during authentication she found a pigment anomaly and filed her doubt away under ambition; (2) she quietly booked Dr. Lorentz for a discreet technical exam *after* the unveiling — the very exam that triggered the theft; (3) in 1921 she stayed silent while the board scapegoated her mentor, Ottilie Marsh. The estrangement is her oldest wound.
- **Lie:** "The provenance was airtight." **Suspicious behavior:** returned to the museum at 23:30 on theft night (fetching her hidden pigment-scrape kit) — seen by a cabbie, reported by Holt.
- **Cleared:** Ch. 5, Archives — full confession. **Motive she appeared to have:** destroy the painting before an exam exposed her authentication. **Why innocent:** she *ordered* the exam; you don't schedule your own exposure.

### Inspector Bram Holt — the honest wall
- 51, weary, fair; treats private detectives as weather. Maxim collection: *"Every locked room has a key with a salary."*
- **Function:** gatekeeper and police-side clue source (cabbie sighting, courier descriptions, Corbin records, the Ch. 6 warrant). His early counter-theory — the insurers over-insured the painting themselves — is a licensed jab at the player's own client.
- **Arc:** territorial → allied. He makes the arrests; the season lets him be right about procedure and wrong about people, twice, graciously.

### Margo Lin — journalist, companion (see §3)
- 29, fast, funny, always one step off to the side. Unlocks locations via Lantern Quarter contacts; delivers recaps as gossip, never exposition.
- **Her one wobble (Ch. 4):** her editor wants the "museum negligence" story *now*; running it would spook the Broker. She sits on it — her choice, on-screen, cheaply heroic and quietly expensive. (Kept: allies don't get secret guilt in this season; they get costs.)

### Victor Casal — the framed suspect
- 45, urbane dealer in "provenance-flexible" goods, resident of Grand Hotel Suite 412.
- **Guilty of:** minor smuggling, cheerful invoice fiction, brokering the 1926 sale without checking the miracle too hard (his margin note: *"provenance thin — but the Belmont wants a miracle"*). **Innocent of:** the theft.
- **Secret:** he *had* dealt with Nachtigall before (coded ledger entries) and, growing suspicious of the 1926 sale, had started quietly re-verifying it — which is exactly why the Broker framed him (forged customs stamp, decoy crate consigned "V.C. Fine Arts").
- **Lie:** "Never dealt with anyone called Nachtigall." Exposed by his own cipher ledger (Ch. 3).
- **Cleared of the theft:** his genuine stamp die has a nicked serif; the customs impression is clean — a forgery (Ch. 3).

### Tobias Finch — the door that opened
- 57, night guard, widower, the museum's gentlest fixture; owes the Anchor's bookmaker exactly 40 crowns.
- **What he did:** admitted "couriers" bearing grand official transfer papers on theft night, accepted a 40-crown "handling fee," and rewrote his 22:00–23:00 log entries. He believed — was *engineered* to believe — it was a confidential insurance removal.
- **Lies:** "All quiet, doors locked" (Ch. 2); "I kept nothing" (he kept his copy of the transfer papers — surrendered at Central Station, Ch. 6, breaking the case).
- **Trace comedy of errors:** his pawn ticket (No. 77, one brass sextant) slid into the duplicate frame's open lining while he helped carry it — the season's inciting clue is the sad accident of a nervous man's coat pocket.

### "Nachtigall" / The Broker — **Ottilie Marsh** (the season's answer)
- 43. Founding conservator of the Belmont (1907–1921), inventor of its patent mounting cradle (1905). Daughter of Aurelian Marsh, harbor cartographer. **The sitter of *The Cartographer's Daughter*** — she is the girl in the painting.
- Nicknamed **"the Belmont's nightingale"** in a 1907 newspaper profile (she sang while working nights in the conservation studio). Dismissed and scapegoated in 1921 over the Marlowe Triptych fake she had *warned against*. Her conservator's seal was never surrendered — margin note in the board minute: *"seal not recovered."*
- Since 1922: **"Nachtigall,"** the night market's untouchable broker of quiet sales and flexible provenance — courteous, precise, oddly protective of the market's small folk (she funds the stallholders' mutual society; this is why the market shields her in Ch. 4).
- **Her signature: she never quite lies.** Every Nachtigall note and every Ottilie sentence is technically true. *"I haven't set foot in the Belmont since 1921"* — true until the last night of the season, when she finally does, to bring the painting home.
- Full motive/method in §6. On stage: masked voice behind the Lattice Stall (Ch. 4); as herself, serving tea and graceful non-lies (Ch. 5); the rooftop (Ch. 6).

### Elias Vane — the dead painter
- 47. Painted *The Cartographer's Daughter* in 1904 in the clocktower workshop, for and of Ottilie. Presumed drowned with the ferry *Aurelia*, 1913 — in truth he missed the sailing (sold his ticket in a bad week, woke up famous and dead, and found the arrangement restful). Wandered the continent as **"Emil Sarto,"** restorer.
- Returned to Marlowe Bay, spring 1926, homesick and grey; found Ottilie; painted the copy of his own painting in the Kestrel Lane loft. **His one vanity:** painting the background harbor map from life in 1926, he "corrected" it — adding the North Mole breakwater, built 1921, eight years after his death and seventeen after the painting's date. The anachronism that unravels everything.
- Left-handed. Grinds his own copper-resinate green with a beeswax binder — "Vane green," documented in his 1904 materials note. Gentle, unhurried, tends the Conservatory's orchids because *"paint taught me patience; flowers examine it."*
- **The season's quiet emotional core:** he never confesses in words. Halloway simply *sees* him — the shears in the left hand, the way he watches light — and lets him keep his silence until the rooftop.

## 5. Victim & Affected Parties

Cozy-noir: no body. The season's victims are institutions, reputations, and two old hearts.

- **The Belmont Museum** — the formal victim: defrauded of 60,000 crowns in 1926 (sold a copy as the original), then robbed of that copy on unveiling eve; its 20th-anniversary triumph turned to scandal.
- **Aldermere Assurance Co.** — the client: exposed on an 80,000-crown policy; hires Halloway by letter (Mr. Pettibone's dry correspondence is a running comic thread).
- **Adele Voss** — career and conscience on the line.
- **Tobias Finch** — coerced through his debts; nearly destroyed by 40 crowns.
- **Victor Casal** — deliberately framed.
- **The painting itself / Vane's legacy** — and, at the bottom of everything, **Ottilie and Elias**, the original victims of 1913 and 1921, who chose a beautiful, illegal remedy.

## 6. The Truth — Full Solution

*(Spoiler master. Everything below is planted before it is used — see §11 and §17.)*

**In 1904,** Elias Vane painted Ottilie Marsh — the harbor cartographer's daughter — in her father's clocktower workshop. The painting stayed in the family; when Aurelian died (1911), Ottilie inherited it and kept it, privately, in the workshop she went on quietly leasing for sixteen years. The art world believed the masterpiece "lost to a private collection."

**In 1913,** Vane was declared drowned with the ferry *Aurelia*. His prices soared. He let the mistake stand and lived abroad as the restorer Emil Sarto.

**In 1921,** the Belmont board — having ignored conservator Ottilie Marsh's written warning that their expensive new Marlowe Triptych was fake — scapegoated her when the fake was exposed. She was dismissed; her junior, Adele Voss, said nothing and kept her career. Ottilie's conservator's seal was never surrendered. By 1922 she was **"Nachtigall,"** the night market's broker — competence weaponized against the establishment that discarded it.

**In 1926,** Vane came home and found her. The Belmont, hungry for a 20th-anniversary coup, was praying for a miracle — so Nachtigall sold them one: "the rediscovered *Cartographer's Daughter*," consigned through the **Aurelia Trust** via Victor Casal, for **60,000 crowns**. But she could not part with the original — her father, her youth, Vane's love, *"the only honest thing I ever owned."* So Vane painted a copy of his own painting. Who alive could contradict his hand? His private joke: the art world that priced his death would pay a fortune for his living brush and call it a relic. His private vanity: he corrected the harbor map in the background — including the **North Mole, built 1921**.

**In September 1927,** Adele — privately uneasy since the authentication — booked **Dr. Lorentz** for a discreet technical examination *after* the unveiling. Her telegram passed through the Central Station telegraph office; Nachtigall's network flagged the name. A pigment exam would destroy the copy, the fraud, and — once anyone traced the copyist — Vane.

**So Ottilie stole her own forgery back.** Forged transfer papers under her retired 1919-era conservator's seal; the Corbin brothers as "couriers"; Tobias Finch — selected because his 40-crown debt was known at the night market — as the duped, paid, plausible door. The framed copy was swapped for a duplicate hollow frame Ottilie built herself and hung on her own patent cradle. A decoy crate (empty), a forged customs stamp bearing Casal's dealer mark, and a lading slip for the SS *Vesper* sent every investigator toward the sea — and neutralized Casal, who had started re-checking the provenance he'd brokered.

A theft makes a painting a legend; a legend never sits for an examination. The fraud would have closed itself — if Finch's pawn ticket hadn't slipped into the frame lining, and if the insurers hadn't sent someone who *looks*.

**The end of it:** when Halloway closes in, Ottilie liquidates (the auction of Vane's studies), books the ferry *Selene* — and then doesn't board. She was never fleeing with the original. She carries it up through the storm to the Belmont's rooftop studio — her old studio — to hang it home in the hollow frame, on her own cradle, before whatever comes. The wren finds the nightingale there, and the case ends the way this season believes cases should: with everything explained, and mercy argued about openly.

## 15. The Finale

Rooftop conservation studio, Belmont Museum, storm night, Oct 29–30. Structure: arrival beat → **S28 Evidence Sweep** (the summation *is* the search: each found story object voices one line of the case) → accusation block → resolution choice (flavor) → epilogue. The GDD's back-to-back-beats exception for the finale applies.

### WHO

**Ottilie Marsh** — founding conservator of the Belmont (1907–1921), inventor of its mounting cradle, daughter of the harbor cartographer, the sitter of *The Cartographer's Daughter*, and for five years the night market's broker **"Nachtigall."** With: **Elias Vane** (alive; painted the 1926 copy), the **Corbin brothers** (hired couriers; arrested by Holt in S27), and **Tobias Finch** (duped, coerced door — legally an accessory, morally a casualty).

### MOTIVE

Layered, in the order she'd admit them: **(1)** she could not surrender the original — her father, her youth, the one honest thing she owned; **(2)** 60,000 crowns, taken from the board that scapegoated her in 1921 — restitution as she accounted it; **(3)** when Adele's secret Lorentz exam threatened the copy, the theft became protection — of the fraud, and of **Vane**, whose living hand a pigment analysis would eventually expose.

### METHOD

Sold the Belmont a Vane-painted 1926 copy as the rediscovered 1904 original (Aurelia Trust, via Casal). When examination loomed: forged transfer papers under her **retired conservator's seal**; exploited Finch's exactly-known 40-crown debt to open the loading dock; swapped the framed copy for a duplicate hollow frame she built herself, hung correctly on her own patent cradle; moved the crate by unscheduled freight tram to the clocktower; laid a sea-trail of lies (empty 9-kg crate, forged Casal-marked customs stamp, "V.C. Fine Arts" consignment) to spend the investigation at the docks and neutralize the one man re-checking her provenance.

### TIMELINE

The §8 table, recited over the storm — 18:05 sealing to 07:40 discovery — with the deep line beneath it: 1904 painted; 1913 "drowned"; 1921 dismissed, seal never surrendered, breakwater built; 1926 copy painted and sold; Sept 1927 the exam booked and flagged; Oct 11 the swap; Oct 29 the homecoming.

### KEY CONTRADICTION

*"You never set foot in the museum — you told me so, and it was true. But the papers that opened its doors were sealed with a stamp the Belmont retired in 1921 — **'seal not recovered'**, the minute says — and the frame that replaced the painting hangs on a cradle re-tensioned by the only hands that ever tensioned it right. The person who did this was inside the Belmont without entering it for six years. There is exactly one person that sentence fits."*
(Exhibits: C31 transfer papers ↔ C26 exemplar & minute; C04 blueprint; C34 the discarded seal itself. Assembled by the player as BR3.)

### CRITICAL EVIDENCE

1. **The anachronism (the fraud):** the museum copy's background map shows the **North Mole, built 1921** (C02 catalog photograph) — absent from the 1904 harbor chart (C06) and the 1904 preparatory study (C24). The "1904 masterpiece" was painted by someone who had seen the 1921 harbor.
2. **The hand (the copyist):** Vane green — copper resinate in beeswax — matching pot, order book, and 1904 materials note (C20, C23, C02); left-handed tool wear.
3. **The seal (the identity):** retired nightingale-watermark seal on the transfer papers (C31), exemplar in the 1918 letter (C26), the physical seal discarded at the frame (C34).
4. **The homecoming (the heart):** the original's 1904 stretcher bar with fresh tack holes (C30), the abandoned ferry ticket, and the brooch with its note (C33) — she wasn't escaping with the painting; she was returning it.

### The confrontation (how it plays)

Ottilie does not run — there is nowhere upward from a rooftop but the truth. She corrects two small errors in Halloway's summation (conservator to the last), confirms the rest, and asks one question: *"Which painting will you give them, detective? The one that's real, or the one that's true?"* Vane arrives, slow through the rain, and answers it for her by signing the copy — *"a Vane, after Vane"* — in front of witnesses. Adele and Holt arrive with the storm's last act. The player's **flavor choice** (GDD-compliant, no branch): press the case *by the book* or *by the heart* — same canonical facts follow; the choice colors Halloway's final two lines and the epilogue's framing sentence only.

**Canonical outcome:** the original is authenticated (Lorentz examines *it* in November — 1904 materials throughout) and re-hung in the duplicate frame she built — the museum quietly keeps her best joinery. The 60,000 crowns are restituted from the auction proceeds and bearer bonds. Aldermere's claim is withdrawn (recovery in full; Pettibone's final letter approaches warmth). Holt arrests the Corbins (double-crossing looters by the end) and processes Ottilie's negotiated surrender; the magistrate weighs grief against fraud — restitution, a short custodial sentence, no violence on the sheet. Finch testifies, is kept on at Adele's insistence, and is moved to daylight hours. Casal is cleared of the theft and fined for everything else, which he pays smiling. Margo publishes everything except one name — see §16.

## 16. The Epilogue

*(Six short panels + a coda; total under four minutes; fully skippable; each panel one location, one character, one sentence of case-summary handwriting beneath. The case closes completely — no thread of THIS mystery is left open.)*

1. **The Belmont, November.** *The Cartographer's Daughter* — the 1904 original — hangs in Gallery One, in Ottilie's frame. Dr. Lorentz's report, one line visible: *"…consistent in all respects with 1904. Remarkable condition. Remarkably loved."* Adele unveils it to a full house and, in her speech, names the conservator who preserved it. First honest applause of her life.
2. **The magistrate's court, a Tuesday.** Ottilie, sentenced — restitution paid, eighteen months, the courtroom oddly gentle. She catalogs the prison library within a week ("the fiction was shelved under *lies*; I've corrected it"). Adele's first letter arrives on a Thursday. The reply begins: *"Dear Adele. Your hanging cord is still two centimeters low."*
3. **The Conservatory, Sunday.** An old man renews the orchids' labels in a careful hand. Small harbor studies, signed *E.V.*, have begun appearing in the Lantern Quarter — "authenticated by A. Voss." He visits the library Sundays. The warden believes he is her solicitor. Nobody corrects the warden.
4. **The Rusty Anchor.** Finch, in daylight now, buys a round with clean money. The Chalk's board lists his name under PAID, framed — Margo's idea of a gift. His sextant sits on the bar's shelf, not the pawnbroker's.
5. **The night market.** Casal, fined and flourishing, sells Margo a "provenance-flexible" hat to replace Halloway's lost one. The stallholders' mutual society finds an anonymous endowment has cleared its books through 1940. Nobody asks.
6. **The Detective's Office.** Margo's front page: **THE HOLLOW FRAME — HOW THE BELMONT'S MASTERPIECE CAME HOME**, every fact of the fraud and the theft — and the copyist named only as *"an unnamed master."* Her trade: the story of the decade for the secret of the century, plus a portrait Vane owes her. Halloway files notebook forty-one, labels it, and feeds Witness (still not Halloway's cat).

**Coda — the Season Two tease (new invitation, not a loose end):** a letter under the door, mirror of the cold open. Aldermere letterhead. Pettibone's hand: *"Halloway — the Marlowe Bay office was quiet for a fortnight, and we find we cannot afford you idle. A carillon in the cathedral quarter has begun ringing changes nobody wrote… and a bell has gone missing that, per our records, does not exist. Clause 9 does not apply. — P."* Halloway opens notebook forty-two, and writes a first word in it — the player's newest vocabulary word, whatever it happens to be.

## 18. Writing Style Guide

- **Beat length:** 45–90 seconds reading, hard cap 90 (GDD). Cadence: beat → search (or puzzle) — never two beats unseparated except the cold open and finale. Target the brief's rhythm: a tight story sip, then play.
- **Humor:** dry, warm, never at the expense of the vulnerable. Sources: Halloway's notebook asides, Margo's velocity, Holt's maxims, Pettibone's clauses, the hat. One laugh-attempt per beat, maximum; the mystery is the star.
- **Patience is a virtue we sell.** No klaxons, no countdowns in prose. Even the Ch. 6 "race" is written as pursuit-by-timetable — urgency from stakes, not sirens.
- **Word-level rules:** clue nouns in beats should echo recently taught vocabulary where natural (the echo rule, §3); never make a plot fact depend on a target-language word; all code-switching obeys the tier gates.
- **Dialogue flavor choices:** two options at key beats, DRY vs. WARM, both leading to identical next states.
- **Case-summary lines** (auto-written to CASE tab): past tense, first person, ≤14 words, one concrete noun each. *"Followed ticket 77 to the Quayside. The pawnbroker remembered a sad man."*
- **Naming:** all persons, ships, firms, and streets are original to this bible (see §19). No borrowings from existing detective IP; no real-world artist estates.
